# Which Search Signals Predict Content Decline? A Signal Analysis of FlyRank's Content Dataset

## Abstract

This study asks which safe, observable content and search signals are associated with a page's visibility declining over time. Using FlyRank's anonymized content dataset (30,000 pages), we labeled pages as "declining" based on their trend direction and tested a hand-built rule-based baseline against a Random Forest classifier trained on content and engagement signals. After discovering and removing a critical data leakage issue — the raw trend-window impression columns nearly perfectly determined the label — the corrected Random Forest reached ROC-AUC 0.767 versus the baseline's 0.58, and correctly identified 98% of its top-50 highest-risk pages as truly declining versus 44% for the baseline. The strongest associated signals were how many days a page shows impressions, its average search position, and content age. These results are observational, not causal, and should guide review priority, not guarantee refresh outcomes.

## Introduction / Problem Statement

Content teams can't manually audit every page for search decline. The decision this work supports: given a large set of pages, which observable signals reliably flag pages worth reviewing before they lose more visibility? This matters because manual review doesn't scale, and a bad prioritization rule wastes review effort on pages that were never actually at risk.

## Data

- Source: FlyRank internship starter dataset, content_refresh_anonymized.csv (30,000 rows, 44 columns — anonymized content_id/client_id, no client names, domains, or URLs used anywhere in this analysis).
- Label: is_declining_label = 1 if trend_direction == 'down' (16,262 of 30,000 rows, ~54%), else 0.
- Excluded from features: identifiers (content_id, client_id), the label source (trend_direction), and — critically — trend_pct and the raw *_last_30d / *_prev_30d columns (see Methodology: leakage).

## Methodology

Baseline (deterministic, rule-based): a weighted score combining visibility (log impressions percentile), freshness risk (days since update), position opportunity, and content-depth gap — weights 0.40/0.30/0.25/0.05, exactly as specified in the repo's scripts/02_baseline_score.py.

Model: Random Forest classifier (200 trees, min_samples_leaf=2), trained on a stratified 70/30 split (21,000 train / 9,000 validation rows).

Leakage audit (important finding): The original feature set included trend_pct. Testing showed trend_pct is a near-exact function (correlation = 0.999999) of impressions_last_30d and impressions_prev_30d — because the label itself is derived from that same percentage change. Leaving these in the feature set produced a perfect model (ROC-AUC = 1.00), which is the textbook sign of leakage, not a good model. All three "last/prev 30-day" trio columns (impressions_last_30d, clicks_last_30d, sessions_last_30d, impressions_prev_30d, clicks_prev_30d, sessions_prev_30d) were removed along with trend_pct, leaving 23 clean numeric features describing a page's broader state (90-day cumulative activity, content characteristics, freshness, position) rather than the exact window the label was computed from.

## Results

| Metric | Baseline | Random Forest |
|---|---|---|
| ROC-AUC | 0.580 | 0.767 |
| Precision@50 | 44.0% | 98.0% |

Confusion matrix (Random Forest, validation set, threshold 0.5):

| | Predicted stable | Predicted declining |
|---|---|---|
| Actually stable | 2,581 | 1,540 |
| Actually declining | 1,174 | 3,705 |

Top signals (permutation importance, ROC-AUC drop when shuffled):

- days_with_impressions — how consistently a page has shown up in search over the window
- avg_position — average ranking position
- content_age_days — how old the content is
- impressions_90d — cumulative visibility
- ctr, char_count, word_count, scroll_rate — secondary signals

Simple correlations with decline (direction matters — this is Lane 1's "effect size" requirement, not just "is there a link"):

- days_with_impressions: +0.19 — pages visible on more days trend more toward decline (plausibly: long-visible pages are further past their peak)
- content_age_days: −0.16 and age_tier_order: −0.16 — somewhat counterintuitively, newer content in this dataset shows a slightly higher decline rate than older content
- word_count: +0.09, days_since_last_update: +0.08 — longer, staler content leans slightly toward decline
- ctr: −0.06 — higher CTR pages lean slightly away from decline

## Limitations & Honest Framing

- These are observational associations, not causal claims — we watched, we did not experiment. Nothing here proves a Google ranking factor, and nothing here proves that refreshing a flagged page will reverse its trend.
- Correlations above are individually weak-to-moderate (all under 0.2); no single signal is a reliable standalone predictor — the model's advantage comes from combining ~23 signals, not any one of them.
- The counterintuitive age finding warrants caution: it may reflect how content was seeded/sampled in this anonymized dataset rather than a general SEO truth.
- The leakage catch above is the single most important finding methodologically — it's a reminder that any "signal" too close to the label's own definition window will inflate results artificially.

## Ranked Recommendations (action playbook)

1. Prioritize review of pages with long-running visibility (days_with_impressions high) rather than only newly-published or freshly-declining ones — established pages showing this pattern deserve monitoring.
2. Cross-check average position alongside impressions — pages slipping in position while remaining visible are a stronger flag than either signal alone.
3. Treat content age and staleness (content_age_days, days_since_last_update) as secondary, supporting evidence, not primary triggers, given the weaker/mixed correlation direction found here.
4. Use the combined model score (not any single metric) to rank review priority — the Random Forest's Precision@50 (98%) vastly outperforms picking by any one heuristic like the baseline's 44%.
5. Always re-run a leakage check (like the one above) before trusting any new feature set on this data.
